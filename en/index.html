In Libreville, the currency of tomorrow enters the lecture hall

Cameroonian expert Armand Gaetan NGUETI leads, at the BGFI Business School, the first Master's-level course ever taught in Central Africa on cryptocurrencies, blockchain, stablecoins and central bank digital currencies. A historic academic moment, at the crossroads of monetary sovereignty and financial innovation.

Read the editorial
01

A first in the academic history of the sub-region

Some course openings feel like affairs of state. From 17 to 22 July 2026, in the lecture halls of the BGFI Business School in Libreville, practising bankers — professionals from the Banking, Finance, Economics, Commerce and Payment Systems tracks — take their seats before a board on which a word is being written that no Central African university had ever placed on a Master 2 curriculum: blockchain. The teaching unit "Cryptocurrencies & Blockchain — Focus on CBDCs & Stablecoins", delivered in the academic LMD format for the 2026-2027 year, is unprecedented in the recent academic history of Gabon and, more broadly, of Central Africa.

Fifteen hours of lectures, tutorials, a workshop and a final project, spread over four days: the format is compact, the ambition immense. For this is not about initiating students into a technological fashion. It is about intellectually equipping those who, tomorrow morning, will return to their counters, their trading rooms, their compliance and payment departments, to confront a question no bank in the world can now evade: what should be done about digital currencies?

02

BBS: the setting of excellence Central Africa has given itself

The choice of venue is no detail — it is a message. Founded in 2008 within the BGFIBank Group, the undisputed banking leader of Central Africa, the BGFI Business School — "the graduate school of management" — rose in 2025 to become the very first grande école in Central Africa certified ISO 21001:2018, the international standard for educational organizations. This certification, which attests to the quality of pedagogical management, the centrality of the learner and the continuous improvement of training programmes, places BBS within a very select circle of African institutions able to speak on equal terms with the world's standards in banking and financial education.

Backed by a group present in a dozen countries, whose some 3,000 employees it trains, and supported by a structured alumni network, BBS is not a school that comments on the banking sector: it is the school that Central Africa's banking sector has given itself — a laboratory where the sub-region's banking industry thinks through its own transformation. That it should be precisely this institution — led by Professor Pamphile Mezui-Mbeng — that opens the way to the teaching of digital assets is no accident: it is the hallmark of houses that prefer to precede history rather than endure it.

By entrusting this inaugural course to an outside expert of continental stature, invited and hosted in Libreville from 16 to 29 July 2026, the school performs an exemplary act of academic governance: seeking out, wherever it may be found, the best available expertise, and placing it at the service of its students.

03

Africa's monetary moment: why now

This course arrives at the exact hour when the monetary history of the sub-region is accelerating. The Bank of Central African States (BEAC) is showing ever keener interest in the evolution of the CFA franc towards a central bank digital currency — a project documented by its communications on the digital CFA franc. At the same time, players in the cryptocurrency sector, the innovation community and users themselves are exerting growing pressure: transfer costs, slow cross-border settlement, compliance requirements, competition from new instruments — so many practical difficulties in the movement of financial assets to which blockchain, stablecoins and altcoins claim to respond.

The global regulatory landscape, for its part, is already in motion: the European MiCA regulation, the American GENIUS Act of 2025, the CEMAC financial market regulation of 2022, pan-African payment systems such as Afreximbank's PAPSS facing the SWIFT network, the pioneering — and critically instructive — experience of Nigeria's eNaira documented by the IMF, and the Bank for International Settlements' work on CBDCs in Africa. Central Africa can no longer content itself with watching: it needs executives able to analyse, compare and decide. That is precisely the competence this course was designed to produce.

04

The man for the moment: a track record that speaks for itself

The choice of expert is anything but incidental — it is, on the contrary, remarkably coherent. Armand Gaetan NGUETI, 37, is one of the most singular figures in African financial technology. Founding President of the Blockchain Association of Cameroon (BAC), Africa President of the Government Blockchain Association (GBA), Founder and CEO of UBTS International Corp (USA) and Africa President of PIPRA Solutions, he holds a distinction unique in the world: he is, to date, the first and only Blockchain Maturity Model (BMM) certified consultant from Africa.

His year 2025 sealed his international stature: a T20 Delegate — the Think20 engagement group — at the G20 Summit in South Africa, he also chairs, from Washington, the GBA's Food Supply Chain Working Group, the first African leader appointed to this strategic position. As Executive Coordinator for the ECCAS region of the Africa Digital Economy Summit (AfriDES), he carries the voice of the eleven countries of extended Central Africa in the continental digital agenda.

But it is perhaps his grounding in the real economy that most impresses practitioners: as the inventor of CAMTRADE PASS, a socio-technological initiative that gave the "Made in Cameroon" label end-to-end traceability through blockchain, AI and IoT, he has demonstrated in the field what theory promises in textbooks — down to the financial inclusion of producers obtaining bank financing on the strength of certified activity histories, without tangible assets. A specialist in blockchain applied to financial inclusion and traceability in Africa, a recognized voice in the public debate on the regulation of digital assets in the CEMAC zone, a trainer of the highest calibre since earning Microsoft Certified Trainer status in Bangalore at just 24, and an executive researcher in management sciences at Université Paul-Valéry Montpellier 3: the man combines the three legitimacies this course demanded — that of the international strategist, that of the ecosystem builder, and that of the educator.

Twenty years of top-tier training delivery, from the lecture halls of Bangalore to the universities of Lubumbashi, from Microsoft programmes to graduate business schools, have forged in him a conviction found on every page of the syllabus he designed in its entirety: technology should not be taught as dogma; it should be taught as an arbitration.

05

Inside the classroom: pedagogical engineering of a master craftsman

The syllabus — designed end to end by the expert, with its dedicated materials — is in itself a lesson in instructional design. Four days, four cognitive levels borrowed from Bloom's taxonomy: knowing and understanding (money, trust, blockchain, Bitcoin), applying and analysing (the cryptocurrency landscape, Ethereum and smart contracts, stablecoin typologies and their risks), analysing and differentiating (CBDCs, their intermediation models, the critical African reading of the eNaira), and finally evaluating and creating (stablecoins versus CBDCs, dollarization, sovereignty, professional synthesis).

Not a single line of code: the choice is deliberate. The approach is "business and payments-driven" — every concept is tied to its concrete effects on payments: costs, speed, settlement, compliance, competition. The tutorials pit card payments against Bitcoin, the stablecoin against SWIFT and PAPSS; the workshop asks: what would a CBDC be for, in Africa and in Europe? And the final project places each student in the chair of the head of payment systems of a commercial bank in the CEMAC zone, required to recommend — or not — the integration of a digital currency from among three options (stablecoin, CBDC, status quo) and to justify the trade-off: costs, speed, inclusion, compliance, sovereignty.

Six competencies structure the learning outcomes, from the fundamentals to the integrative capacity to formulate and defend a contextualized professional recommendation, by way of payments analysis, regulatory assessment (AML/CFT, consumer protection, BEAC and MiCA frameworks) and the strategic appreciation of monetary sovereignty and the geopolitics of payments. The assessment itself respects LMD alignment: an ungraded diagnostic quiz, continuous assessment at 40%, and the final project — written grade plus oral defence — at 60%. A demanding bibliography accompanies the whole, from Nakamoto (2008) to the BIS Papers on CBDCs in Africa, from the IMF working paper on the eNaira to the 2025 Chainalysis reports, through to the MiCA texts, the GENIUS Act and the CEMAC financial market regulation.

To develop "the critical thinking needed to distinguish the real value of these technologies from the hype": the phrase, taken from the course description, could serve as the motto of the entire endeavour. This is far, very far, from crypto evangelism. This is the training of decision-makers.

Training decision-makers
06

What this course changes — and what it heralds

The students of this session are not novices in search of retraining: they are serving bankers, holding varied responsibilities across the sub-region's banking architecture. That is what gives this July week its systemic reach. Every competency acquired in Libreville will, from the very next week, flow into a risk department, a compliance committee, a payments division, a bank's strategic thinking. The course reinforces precisely the graduate profile the LMD system requires of Master's degree holders in banking, finance, economics and commerce: competencies directly deployable with banks, fintechs, Mobile Money operators, regulators — BEAC, COBAC, COSUMAF — and regional institutions.

Consider the convergence of symbols: the first ISO-certified grande école of Central Africa, backed by the sub-region's leading banking group, entrusts the first Master's course devoted to digital currencies to the continent's first BMM-certified consultant — at the precise moment when the regional central bank is exploring the digital future of the CFA franc. There are alignments that owe nothing to chance and everything to vision.

This editorialist will refrain from prophesying the fate of stablecoins or the timetable of a possible CEMAC CBDC — the course itself teaches, precisely, to distrust easy certainties. But one thing is settled: on the evening of 22 July 2026, Central Africa will count a cohort of banking executives able to analyse, compare and recommend, as professionals of payment systems, on the most strategic dossier of the monetary decade. This is how financial centres are built: not by decree, but by knowledge. And this is how a region ceases to be a spectator of its monetary destiny and becomes, at last, its operator.
